Network effects are the gold standard of defensibility. A product becomes more valuable as more people use it. Once achieved, network effects create switching costs that protect against competition. Our November 2025 research explores how to design for and achieve network effects.


## Direct vs Indirect Network Effects

Direct network effects occur when users directly benefit from other users: social networks, communication tools, multiplayer games. More users means more value for each user.

Indirect network effects occur through intermediaries: more riders attract more drivers which attract more riders. Platforms exhibit indirect effects through their marketplace dynamics.

According to [a16z research](https://a16z.com/), companies with network effects represent the majority of value created in technology because of their defensibility.


## The Critical Mass Challenge

Network effects only work at scale. Before critical mass, the product has negative network effects: too few users makes it less useful than alternatives with more users.

The chicken-and-egg problem requires solving both sides simultaneously. Common strategies include subsidizing one side, seeding with supply, or focusing on a single concentrated market.


## Local vs Global Networks

Not all networks need global scale. Some networks operate locally: restaurant reviews in a city, dating in a region, professional networking in an industry.

Local network effects can be achieved with smaller absolute numbers. The strategy is winning individual markets completely rather than spreading thin globally.


## Defensibility Through Data

Beyond user-to-user network effects, data network effects create defensibility. More usage generates more data which improves the product which attracts more usage.

AI products particularly benefit from data network effects. The product with more training data produces better results which attracts more users which generates more data.
